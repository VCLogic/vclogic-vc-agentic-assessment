# Charles Reasoning-Fidelity Audit

## Scope and comparison rule

This audit compares four things for Dig, Pepper, Harper Wilde, and Nectir:

1. Charles Hudson's observable questions, comments, decision, and explanation in the complete episode transcript.
2. The earlier Codex-orchestrated two-phase output (Phase 1 rationales and Phase 2 decision synthesis).
3. The new leakage-safe LangGraph/Luna Phase 1 rationales.
4. The new LangGraph/Luna Phase 2 weighting and decision.

“Historical Codex” describes the earlier workflow orchestrated through Codex; the VC
judgments inside those artifacts were generated by the configured Luna/Sol agents. It
does not mean that a Codex foundation model directly produced every judgment.

The actual label is Charles's observable pitch-window investment intent. Under the
project's chosen rule, an expressed willingness to invest subject to a routine or
minor diligence condition remains **In**. Consequently, Nectir is **In**, not Out.

The inference input was the A-cut. It contains founder speech only: no Charles turns,
no other investor comments, and no verdict. This prevents outcome leakage, but it also
means the model cannot observe information that changed Charles's mind during the
panel conversation—most importantly, the other investors' market-size feedback in
Pepper.

|---|---:|---:|---:|---|---|
| Dig | In | In 0.62 | In 0.53 | Correct in both | Good overall; both miss exit-alignment nuance |
| Pepper | In | Out 0.34 | Out 0.22 | Missed in both | New run is worse because it invents a fatal conflict |
| Harper Wilde | Out | In 0.62 | Out 0.10 | New outcome corrected | New result is right mainly for the wrong controlling reason |
| Nectir | In | In 0.61 | Out 0.24 | New run regressed | Phase 1 is strong; Phase 2 contradicts Charles and its own Phase 1 |

For the requested Dig/Pepper/Harper comparison, the new workflow is correct on 2/3,
versus 1/3 for the historical workflow. Across all four cases, both workflows are
correct on 2/4, but they fail differently. The historical workflow has higher In
recall; the new workflow correctly rejects Harper but incorrectly rejects Nectir.

Ranking tells a different story. The new any-check likelihood orders the episodes as
Dig, Nectir, Pepper, Harper. With the corrected labels, all three Ins rank above the
Out (average precision 1.0 in this deliberately selected four-case diagnostic).

## Episode 68: Dig

### What Charles actually attended to

Charles praised the opening, asked how many dog owners viewed pet compatibility as a
must-have, and then made a deliberately small investment. His explanation combined:

- strong founder communication and energy;
- a specific customer insight and evidence that the founder was “on to something”;
- concern that dating was consolidated and might not produce a huge outcome;
- positive category pattern recognition from Fuzzy and Dog Parker;
- belief that business-model possibilities improve with audience scale;
- alignment with a plausible early acquisition, which justified a $10K rather than
  standard fund check;
- mild valuation concern, but not a veto.

His post-pitch explanation is especially revealing: he described Precursor as
“people pickers” and framed the question as the right amount to back this person,
despite agreeing that the company might not become gigantic.

### Historical Codex-orchestrated run

The historical Phase 1 recovered founder-market fit, execution, communication,
problem validation, adoption, venture-scale concern, business-model uncertainty,
competition, fund fit, and investor experience bias. Phase 2 used the founder and
early adoption evidence to authorize a sub-$100K exploratory check while rejecting a
standard check.

This is close to Charles's actual structure. Its main omissions were the explicit
early-acquisition alignment and the fact that valuation was negotiable rather than a
general fund-mandate problem. It also emphasized missing retention and monetization
metrics more than Charles did in his stated explanation.

### New LangGraph/Luna run

New Phase 1 extracted 15 rationales. It recovered the actual positive founder,
problem, adoption, acquisition-cost, execution, and communication signals, as well as
the actual competition, defensibility, monetization, and venture-scale concerns. It
also correctly classified Fuzzy and Dog Parker as routine portfolio adjacency, not a
conflict.

Phase 2 recommended an exploratory check below $100K and rejected a standard check.
That matches Charles's actual sizing behavior. However, it incorrectly treated the
$1.5M total round as conflicting with Precursor's mandate. Charles's $10K offer is
direct evidence that total round size did not block a small participation.

### Fidelity judgment

- Outcome: correct in both systems.
- Rationale extraction: strong in both; the new extraction is more granular.
- Decision synthesis: directionally faithful in both.
- Main miss: neither system explicitly reconstructs the small-check/early-exit
  alignment that Charles used to justify investing despite limited venture scale.

## Episode 83: Pepper

### What Charles actually attended to

Charles asked how customers were found, challenged the size of the small-cup segment,
and explicitly revised his market-size belief after hearing the other panelists. He
said the competitive strategy had to be a different game—organic, bottom-up
community—and asked whether Pepper could own the category before funded incumbents
responded. He then investigated use of capital, product sequencing, and where an
investor could help.

His final explanation identified the controlling positive rationales directly:

- liking the founder;
- willingness to update upward on market size;
- capital efficiency;
- a sharply defined, easily identifiable niche customer;
- a focused beachhead: spend the next two years defending the core bra category;
- a small $25K check, with ordinary diligence still to do.

Charles did not treat True & Co as a fatal conflict. He invested despite the category
adjacency.

### Historical Codex-orchestrated run

Historical Phase 1 recovered the problem, focused market-entry wedge, market-size
uncertainty, differentiation, economics, go-to-market, founder motivation and
execution, financing fit, and True & Co adjacency. Importantly, it classified the
portfolio issue as neutral and nonfatal.

Phase 2 nevertheless returned Out because it demanded proof of a $100M revenue
bridge, cohort economics, scalable paid acquisition, team completeness, and resolved
terms. This is too strict for Charles's observed small-check behavior. The model
recognized most of the ingredients but failed to combine capital efficiency, a crisp
beachhead, and founder appeal into an exploratory In.

### New LangGraph/Luna run

New Phase 1 again recovered the real problem, custom product work, uncertain
defensibility, market size, promising early economics, channel questions,
coachability, capital intensity, competition, and the Urban Outfitters reorder.

The decisive failure was `portfolio_conflict_constraint`. Phase 1 called True & Co a
“material potential overlap,” and Phase 2 elevated it to a fatal constraint that no
exploratory check could cure. This is directly contradicted by Charles's actual
investment. The model also failed to reproduce Charles's belief update after Maia and
Elizabeth validated the segment size.

### Fidelity judgment

- Outcome: missed by both systems.
- Historical rationale extraction: fairly close; synthesis too conservative.
- New rationale extraction: broad, but the conflict classification is wrong.
- New decision synthesis: poor; the false conflict controls the verdict.
- Important data limitation: the A-cut excludes the panel comments that visibly
  changed Charles's market-size assessment. A pitch-only model could infer that he
  might update, but it cannot observe the actual social-information event.

## Episode 20: Harper Wilde

### What Charles actually attended to

Charles acknowledged unusually strong customer-pain understanding. His decisive
reason for passing was not a formal portfolio conflict or simply missing metrics. He
said he did not share the founders' vision for the brand principles and brand anchor,
and did not think they saw the opportunity the same way.

After the founders left, he connected the case to True & Co and the repeated failure
of bra challengers to dislodge a dominant incumbent. That was category pattern
recognition and competitive inertia, not an assertion that he was ethically barred
from investing.

### Historical Codex-orchestrated run

Historical Phase 1 recovered customer pain, founder-market fit, motivation,
resourceful execution, communication, product thesis, competitive inertia,
differentiation risk, repeatability, venture scale, and business-model uncertainty.

It missed the decisive founder-investor vision-alignment rationale. Phase 2 then
overrode the negative market and differentiation evidence with a general pre-seed
optionality rule and predicted In. In effect, it treated thoughtful founders plus a
cheap experiment as sufficient, while Charles required alignment on the brand's core
meaning.

### New LangGraph/Luna run

New Phase 1 recovered problem insight, authentic motivation, resourceful execution,
missing adoption results, differentiation risk, business-model uncertainty, and
market-size uncertainty. `product_differentiation_concern` partially approximates
Charles's brand-anchor concern, but it does not capture the interpersonal thesis
misalignment he explicitly named.

Phase 2 predicted Out, but made the unresolved True & Co overlap a fatal portfolio
constraint and the primary reason for rejection. That is not what Charles said or
did. The correct outcome is therefore only partially reasoning-faithful: competitive
and differentiation concerns are relevant, but the controlling mechanism is wrong.

### Fidelity judgment

- Outcome: historical system wrong; new system correct.
- Rationale extraction: both capture the broad positive and competitive evidence.
- Decisive-rationale recall: both miss explicit brand-principle/vision alignment.
- New decision synthesis: correct outcome, wrong primary cause.

## Episode 127: Nectir

### What Charles actually attended to

Charles asked why customers did not buy, how many people were on the team, where new
sales hires would create leverage, and whether Slack had changed educational pricing.
These questions map directly to sales objections, team capacity, go-to-market
scalability, competition, and product economics.

He then emphasized extensive edtech portfolio experience and expressed interest in
participating. His only stated caveat was checking Campus for a possible legacy
feature overlap. He simultaneously said he had high confidence that the feature was
not essential and specified a desired $50K–$100K check. Under the project's current
rule, this is an In with a routine condition.

Crucially, Charles heard that Nectir was raising a $3M seed round and still expressed
willingness to invest. Total round size therefore cannot be treated as a fatal
mandate mismatch in this case.

### Historical Codex-orchestrated run

Historical Phase 1 recovered authentic motivation, founder-market fit, execution,
communication, problem validation, adoption, go-to-market, repeatability, venture
scale, differentiation, valuation/stage fit, timing, and business model. Phase 2
predicted an exploratory In based on paid deployment, renewals, cohort onboarding,
LMS usage, and founder execution.

This decision is correct and its positive reasoning is substantially aligned with
Charles's interest. However, it missed the actual explicit Campus conflict caveat and
Charles's positive edtech pattern recognition. It also overemphasized entry-price
mismatch, although it correctly treated that as check-limiting rather than fatal.

### New LangGraph/Luna run

New Phase 1 is the strongest extraction of the four cases. It recovered founder
motivation and execution, paid validation, LMS advantage, sales-cycle questions,
repeatability, growth risk, outcome measurement, timing, team depth, fundraising
demand, investor alignment, and portfolio overlap. It correctly judged the portfolio
overlap minor/routine rather than material.

Phase 2 then contradicted both the actual episode and its own Phase 1. It made the $3M
round a fatal `stage_valuation_mismatch` and returned Out, even though Charles offered
precisely the kind of small check the framework claims to model. The Phase 2 error is
not a lack of rationale extraction; it is a faulty decision rule.

### Fidelity judgment

- Outcome: historical system correct; new system wrong.
- Historical extraction: strong but misses the explicit conflict caveat.
- New extraction: very strong and includes the actual conflict structure.
- New synthesis: poor; it converts total round size into a veto despite direct
  contrary behavioral evidence.

## Cross-case diagnosis

### 1. Total round size is being confused with Charles's check size

The new system treats $1.5M or $3M rounds as mandate conflicts because the wiki
describes Precursor's preferred pre-seed construct. The episodes show a different
behavioral rule: Charles can participate in a larger financing with a small check.
Dig ($10K), Pepper ($25K), and Nectir ($50K–$100K intent) all demonstrate this.
Round size should usually limit check tier or confidence, not automatically block an
any-check decision.

### 2. Portfolio adjacency is being promoted to conflict without evidence

True & Co adjacency did not prevent Charles from investing in Pepper and was not his
stated reason for rejecting Harper Wilde. Campus was the only explicit conflict check
in these four episodes, and Charles framed it as likely routine. A portfolio concern
should become fatal only with evidence of direct product/customer/channel overlap or
an explicit legitimate grievance—not merely a shared category label.

### 3. Phase 1 is generally better than Phase 2

The new investigator often finds the relevant evidence. The largest errors occur
when synthesis assigns controlling power: fatal conflict in Pepper and Harper, and
fatal round mismatch in Nectir. Improving extraction alone will not solve these
errors; the decision policy and constraint severity calibration must change.

### 4. The system misses investor-specific exception logic

Charles sometimes buys a small option when he likes the founder and sees a specific
wedge even though venture scale is unresolved. Dig and Pepper show that the decision
is not “all standard underwriting questions answered.” It is “is there enough
company-specific evidence, founder quality, and fit to justify the right-sized
check?” The historical system captured this for Dig and Nectir but applied it
inconsistently to Pepper and too permissively to Harper.

### 5. Pitch-only evaluation omits causal panel information

Pepper demonstrates genuine social updating: Charles explicitly credits fellow
panelists with changing his market-size belief. Because the A-cut contains only
founder speech, neither model observes that event. This does not invalidate the
pitch-only forecasting task, but it means outcome error can reflect missing decision
inputs rather than poor imitation alone. A separate leakage-safe panel-replay task
would be needed to measure whether the agent can reproduce Charles after seeing the
same pre-verdict interaction.

## Recommended changes before scaling

1. Split **round size**, **entry valuation**, **Charles check size**, and **stage** into
   separate rationale fields. Do not infer a fatal fund mismatch from total round
   size alone.
2. Require affirmative overlap evidence before `portfolio_conflict_constraint` can
   be material or fatal. Category adjacency defaults to noted/routine.
3. Add a specific `founder_investor_vision_alignment` or `brand_thesis_alignment`
   rationale to capture Harper Wilde's decisive failure mode.
4. Require Phase 2 to preserve Phase 1 severity: it may not promote neutral/routine
   overlap into a fatal constraint without new evidence.
5. Calibrate the exploratory-check rule around company-specific de-risking and
   right-sized participation, while preventing generic founder enthusiasm from
   overriding a demonstrated thesis misalignment.
6. Report two separate evaluation settings in future work: pitch-only forecasting
   and pre-verdict panel replay. Do not mix their interpretations.

These changes should be developed on training/development episodes only and then
frozen before scoring untouched episodes. The four cases here are now error-analysis
examples and should not be reused as an unbiased holdout after the policy is revised.
