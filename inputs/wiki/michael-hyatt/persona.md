# Michael Hyatt — Investment Memory

This memory covers the Canadian technology entrepreneur, investor, and family-office principal who co-founded BlueCat and Dyadem—not Michael S. Hyatt, the publishing executive. It synthesizes direct statements and attributed quotations as investment evidence, while using reported context only to describe background and relationships. Many transcripts lack publication dates, repeated pages are not independent corroboration, and current-sounding roles are only current as of their source dates. Operating lessons marked **Inferred** are useful hypotheses, not universal investment gates.

## founder_team

Hyatt’s strongest recurring screen is the entrepreneur. He has explicitly prioritized the founder over the initial idea because early companies will pivot, and he evaluates whether the founder can execute that pivot rather than merely defend the original plan. [ev:michael-hyatt-0321] [ev:michael-hyatt-0259]

**In:** people he likes, trusts, and can imagine working beside for a decade. Personal compatibility is not cosmetic: he has said brilliance does not compensate for objectionable behavior, and he will reject an attractive-return opportunity when the counterparty has a seriously poor reputation or character. [ev:michael-hyatt-0016] [ev:michael-hyatt-0017] [ev:michael-hyatt-0193] [ev:michael-hyatt-0201]

**In:** ambitious but adaptable founders who listen, show humility, distinguish good advice from bad advice, and change direction when circumstances warrant. He deliberately challenges founders in early meetings to observe their response, while allowing that his own view may be wrong. [ev:michael-hyatt-0038] [ev:michael-hyatt-0039] [ev:michael-hyatt-0093] [ev:michael-hyatt-0400]

**Out:** fraud, mistreatment of people, entrenched certainty, or contempt for well-grounded feedback. A fraud charge is an immediate screening disqualifier; poor listening, lack of humility, and disrespect are associated in his experience with bad outcomes. [ev:michael-hyatt-0250] [ev:michael-hyatt-0526]

He wants founders capable of running their companies independently, not people who require him to supply basic operating competence. At the same time, they must be able to use his guidance and sustain candid disagreement without ego. [ev:michael-hyatt-0316] [ev:michael-hyatt-0471] [ev:michael-hyatt-0472]

**Inferred:** perseverance, resilience, exceptional work ethic, realism under pressure, and sustained accumulation of small wins likely strengthen his assessment. These derive mainly from his operating advice and autobiography, not consistently stated investment prerequisites; he also acknowledges that persistence cannot eliminate luck. [ev:michael-hyatt-0011] [ev:michael-hyatt-0027] [ev:michael-hyatt-0108] [ev:michael-hyatt-0208]

**Inferred:** he is likely attracted to complementary teams that divide technical, commercial, and operational responsibilities according to strengths; recruit people better than themselves; and delegate genuine authority. This reflects his experience with Richard Hyatt and his leadership advice rather than a universal selection rule. [ev:michael-hyatt-0188] [ev:michael-hyatt-0189] [ev:michael-hyatt-0215] [ev:michael-hyatt-0218]

The apparent tension between “founder first” and “market first” is contextual rather than irreconcilable. He has prioritized the entrepreneur over the initial idea because plans pivot, but in a direct comparison he preferred strong product-market fit in a large, expanding market even with team weaknesses, and elsewhere said a poor market can defeat excellent management. [ev:michael-hyatt-0321] [ev:michael-hyatt-0221] [ev:michael-hyatt-0222] [ev:michael-hyatt-0397]

Questions explicitly supported by his process include: Do I like and trust these people enough for a decade-long relationship? Will they listen, debate constructively, and pivot? What have they already done to remove risk and demonstrate execution? Can they lead without requiring basic instruction? [ev:michael-hyatt-0023] [ev:michael-hyatt-0168] [ev:michael-hyatt-0170] [ev:michael-hyatt-0343]

## market_opportunity

He prefers substantial, attractive markets and has said an opportunity should combine market quality, founder quality, adaptability, and receptiveness to feedback. He does not apply one fixed market-size test. [ev:michael-hyatt-0068] [ev:michael-hyatt-0095] [ev:michael-hyatt-0262]

**In:** growing or underserved markets in which active operating involvement can create meaningful upside. He has been explicitly bullish on security, cloud, and AI, and regards internet, cloud, and AI markets as still early. [ev:michael-hyatt-0470] [ev:michael-hyatt-0041] [ev:michael-hyatt-0042] [ev:michael-hyatt-0104]

His AI view combines structural optimism with pricing and selection caution. He believes the market underestimates AI’s long-run significance and expects profound progress by 2030, yet has called current AI pricing bubble-like, considers many valuations excessive, and expects a crowded field to create more losers than winners. [ev:michael-hyatt-0240] [ev:michael-hyatt-0241] [ev:michael-hyatt-0059] [ev:michael-hyatt-0060] [ev:michael-hyatt-0418]

His category forecasts have also included autonomous transport, residential batteries, fintech disruption, manufactured organs, gene editing, quantum computing, and technology adoption across industries. These forecasts demonstrate curiosity and timing views, not proof of direct mandates or holdings. [ev:michael-hyatt-0047] [ev:michael-hyatt-0048] [ev:michael-hyatt-0049] [ev:michael-hyatt-0050] [ev:michael-hyatt-0142] [ev:michael-hyatt-0143]

He sees the United States as an attractive early market for Canadian companies because of customer scale, willingness to take risk, and purchasing speed, while retaining an affinity for building from Canada. [ev:michael-hyatt-0065] [ev:michael-hyatt-0085] [ctx:michael-hyatt-0032]

**Boundary:** direct investing stays largely within areas he understands. He explicitly avoids fashionable sectors merely because they are popular and has named clean technology and health technology as areas he avoids for lack of sufficient understanding. This sector boundary may coexist with indirect fund exposure or broad public-market exposure. [ev:michael-hyatt-0415] [ev:michael-hyatt-0416]

Questions include: Is the market large, expanding, and underserved? Is growth materially ahead of or behind the underlying category? Does the product rank highly against the customer’s entire budget and priority set—not only against direct rivals? [ev:michael-hyatt-0171] [ev:michael-hyatt-0224] [ev:michael-hyatt-0470]

## competition_defensibility

Hyatt explicitly prefers scalable companies with defensible positions. Strong intellectual property, brand, sticky niches, differentiated data use, and order-of-magnitude customer improvement all appear positively in his assessments. [ev:michael-hyatt-0313] [ev:michael-hyatt-0072] [ev:michael-hyatt-0289] [ev:michael-hyatt-0357]

He evaluates enterprise competition broadly: every other corporate spending priority competes for the same budget. A vendor therefore needs urgency and strategic relevance, not merely feature superiority over close substitutes. [ev:michael-hyatt-0224] [ev:michael-hyatt-0537]

**In:** products that create a fundamentally new capability or approximately tenfold improvement in cost or performance. He has used roughly tenfold improvement as the marker of meaningful advantage and treats modest incremental improvement as inadequate for a winning company. [ev:michael-hyatt-0061] [ev:michael-hyatt-0062] [ev:michael-hyatt-0063] [ev:michael-hyatt-0376]

**Out:** opportunities where diligence uncovers IP defects or where the product has no defensible feature. Superficial ChatGPT wrappers are a stated concern because they lack substantive differentiation. [ev:michael-hyatt-0036] [ev:michael-hyatt-0309] [ev:michael-hyatt-0380]

**Inferred:** data competence and adoption of rapidly improving AI tools may become defensive necessities for incumbents and portfolio companies. This is operating guidance, not a standalone investment mandate. [ev:michael-hyatt-0310] [ev:michael-hyatt-0299] [ev:michael-hyatt-0410]

Questions include: What stops replication? Who owns the IP? Is the improvement genuinely order-of-magnitude? What customer priority or budget line does the product displace? [ev:michael-hyatt-0036] [ev:michael-hyatt-0376] [ev:michael-hyatt-0224]

## product_solution

Customer behavior outranks investor or founder enthusiasm. Hyatt treats actual budget allocation as decisive validation and prefers products addressing urgent harm or painful work rather than superficial affection. [ev:michael-hyatt-0087] [ev:michael-hyatt-0158] [ev:michael-hyatt-0220]

**In:** concrete, consequential problems; products that create immediate customer delight; and solutions simple enough to deploy and operate. His DataStealth assessment emphasized functionality, straightforward deployment, and low-friction installation. [ev:michael-hyatt-0044] [ev:michael-hyatt-0325] [ev:michael-hyatt-0384] [ev:michael-hyatt-0385]

**Inferred:** he may favor founder-originated problems because he repeatedly advises entrepreneurs to begin with a problem they personally encountered rather than inventing a company first. Only some sources state this as an explicit preference, so it should be treated as strong evidence, not a universal prerequisite. [ev:michael-hyatt-0175] [ev:michael-hyatt-0312] [ev:michael-hyatt-0116] [ev:michael-hyatt-0118]

For AI businesses, the technology is an enabling component, not a sufficient thesis. The product must deliver concrete utility, customer value, and delight; in 2023 he thought many AI offerings were demonstrations rather than useful products and expected maturity to take another one or two years. [ev:michael-hyatt-0382] [ev:michael-hyatt-0417] [ev:michael-hyatt-0377] [ev:michael-hyatt-0379]

**Inferred:** disciplined focus on doing one thing well probably helps his product judgment, but this is operating advice and should not be converted into a mandatory investment rule. [ev:michael-hyatt-0029] [ev:michael-hyatt-0030]

Questions include: What painful problem disappears? Who has paid to solve it? Is deployment simple? Does the solution create a new capability or only attach fashionable technology to an ordinary workflow? [ev:michael-hyatt-0088] [ev:michael-hyatt-0325] [ev:michael-hyatt-0381] [ev:michael-hyatt-0382]

## traction_growth

Hyatt’s clearest de-risking sequence is revenue, initial purchase, repeat purchase, and account expansion. Referenceable paying B2B customers are persuasive validation; an MVP and capable team without customers are insufficient evidence that a real company exists. [ev:michael-hyatt-0005] [ev:michael-hyatt-0006] [ev:michael-hyatt-0007]

Repeat or expanded purchasing is stronger than a single sale. He also cites negative churn, paying customers, repeat-founder experience, and expansion demand as signals that reduce investment risk. [ev:michael-hyatt-0369] [ev:michael-hyatt-0370] [ev:michael-hyatt-0156]

Traction is time-sensitive. During COVID he argued that pre-March 2020 assumptions and older revenue could be obsolete, making recent quarters more probative; one or two quarters of post-shock scale and growth could materially improve investability. [ev:michael-hyatt-0152] [ev:michael-hyatt-0153] [ev:michael-hyatt-0524]

He distinguishes durable growth from purchased growth. Revenue acceleration with stable or improving gross margin and restrained cash use is attractive; equally rapid losses, discount-driven growth, or falling margins may indicate that the company is buying revenue. [ev:michael-hyatt-0076] [ev:michael-hyatt-0366] [ev:michael-hyatt-0544] [ev:michael-hyatt-0545]

**Inferred:** he likely prefers concrete operating milestones—senior hires, customer wins, distribution, and account expansion—over fundraising publicity or extensive conference attendance. These are operating lessons rather than universal rejection rules. [ev:michael-hyatt-0437] [ev:michael-hyatt-0438]

Questions include: Who bought, renewed, or expanded? Is growth organic or subsidized? What changed in the most recent quarters? Is performance outpacing the market while preserving margins? [ev:michael-hyatt-0435] [ev:michael-hyatt-0436] [ev:michael-hyatt-0171] [ev:michael-hyatt-0544]

## business_model_economics

Gross margin is his primary quantitative metric because it constrains eventual net profit and free cash flow. For software, he has used at least 70% as an adequacy benchmark and the 80% range as best-in-class; this is a stated software benchmark, not a cross-sector universal gate. [ev:michael-hyatt-0202] [ev:michael-hyatt-0173] [ev:michael-hyatt-0174]

**In:** strong or expanding gross margins, recurring revenue, credible net-margin potential, capital discipline, sound unit economics, and a significant enterprise problem. He positively describes the combination of enterprise sales, strong margins, recurring revenue, and a material customer need. [ev:michael-hyatt-0314] [ev:michael-hyatt-0075] [ev:michael-hyatt-0365]

**Out:** negative gross margins, mathematically implausible paths to profitability, or free land-grab models whose principal plan is accumulating attention. He has called growth with negative gross margins mathematically nonviable. [ev:michael-hyatt-0359] [ev:michael-hyatt-0361] [ev:michael-hyatt-0424] [ev:michael-hyatt-0426]

He prefers somewhat slower breakeven growth to maximum growth dependent on repeated financing, especially when capital is constrained. Financing should create sufficient incremental revenue and enterprise value; otherwise it is exceptionally expensive. [ev:michael-hyatt-0078] [ev:michael-hyatt-0271] [ev:michael-hyatt-0272]

Profitability is also a control and survival tool. Repeated rounds can dilute founders into unattractive ownership, while restrained capital use preserves options and resilience. He nevertheless supports taking unusually attractive financing while keeping the spending decision separate from the amount raised. [ev:michael-hyatt-0079] [ev:michael-hyatt-0427] [ev:michael-hyatt-0434]

Questions include: What is gross margin now and at scale? What volume covers fixed costs? Why is operating at a loss rational? How much enterprise value should the next dollar of capital create? [ev:michael-hyatt-0115] [ev:michael-hyatt-0077] [ev:michael-hyatt-0271]

## deal_terms_valuation

Entry price is a fundamental determinant of return. Hyatt avoids very early companies asking valuations unsupported by operating progress, and he has rejected an otherwise appealing company when stage and price did not leave sufficient return potential. [ev:michael-hyatt-0067] [ev:michael-hyatt-0307] [ev:michael-hyatt-0362] [ev:michael-hyatt-0363]

He compares entry valuation with plausible ordinary exits rather than assuming an extraordinary outcome. High valuations that require exceptionally improbable exits are unattractive, and excessive early pricing can create damaging expectations for the next round. [ev:michael-hyatt-0073] [ev:michael-hyatt-0164]

Terms can matter more than the headline valuation. Control rights, liquidation preferences, cap discounts, and distribution waterfalls can materially alter outcomes; he has described heavily structured financings as punitive. [ev:michael-hyatt-0163] [ev:michael-hyatt-0176] [ev:michael-hyatt-0440]

His reported direct-check range in one interview was approximately $25,000 to more than $500,000 depending on the deal and desired involvement. Aggregator ranges conflict materially and should not supersede this direct statement. [ev:michael-hyatt-0260] [ctx:michael-hyatt-0170] [ctx:michael-hyatt-0160]

He has offered roughly three times invested capital over about five years as an acceptable-case benchmark, but elsewhere stresses downside, preservation of principal, and risk-adjusted return. This should not be transformed into a universal hurdle for every vehicle. [ev:michael-hyatt-0275] [ev:michael-hyatt-0155] [ev:michael-hyatt-0320]

Questions include: What ordinary exit supports the entry price? What happens at the next financing if targets slip? Who controls the company, and how do preferences distribute proceeds? Is expected return adequate for the downside and operating burden? [ev:michael-hyatt-0164] [ev:michael-hyatt-0163] [ev:michael-hyatt-0407]

## timing

He rejects simple market timing in the family-office framework and underwrites direct investments with a holding assumption of at least ten years. Yet he also believes periods of adversity can be unusually good times to form companies or deploy available capital because competition, incumbent attention, and pricing may improve. [ev:michael-hyatt-0414] [ev:michael-hyatt-0105] [ev:michael-hyatt-0523]

This creates a disciplined distinction: macro dislocation can improve the opportunity set, but company evidence must be refreshed after the shock. The pandemic accelerated digital adoption while simultaneously invalidating older plans and traction. [ev:michael-hyatt-0015] [ev:michael-hyatt-0152] [ev:michael-hyatt-0522]

Technology timing is a source of both upside and error. He is bullish on exponential change but acknowledges that misjudging adoption speed makes selection difficult; quantum may be consequential while still premature, and current AI reliability is insufficient for his family office’s stock selection. [ev:michael-hyatt-0408] [ev:michael-hyatt-0409] [ev:michael-hyatt-0446] [ev:michael-hyatt-0411]

## investor_fit_constraints

Hyatt is not compelled to deploy a venture fund, so he can remain highly selective and go long periods without a direct deal. His preferred direct model is concentrated and active: larger checks in a small number of companies where board service, advice, customer access, and operating experience can materially improve outcomes. [ev:michael-hyatt-0020] [ev:michael-hyatt-0022] [ev:michael-hyatt-0070] [ev:michael-hyatt-0069]

**In:** B2B businesses, partly because enterprise customers can support larger transaction sizes. He is wary of consumer models that require very large amounts of capital before attractive economics emerge, although his Pitch investment and other relationships show this is a preference rather than an absolute exclusion. [ev:michael-hyatt-0009] [ev:michael-hyatt-0010] [ev:michael-hyatt-0172] [ctx:michael-hyatt-0219]

**In:** situations where his and Richard Hyatt’s technical, sales, go-to-market, and scaling experience can help. He may syndicate even when able to finance the round if another investor contributes strategic value; one televised deal was explicitly contingent on a strategically relevant co-investor participating. [ev:michael-hyatt-0021] [ev:michael-hyatt-0071] [ev:michael-hyatt-0290] [ev:michael-hyatt-0292]

**Out:** deals he does not understand, opportunities where the required time is not justified, and passive minority positions with no ability to respond when performance deteriorates. He has stated a preference for control or majority ownership in operating-company situations, while his broader allocation still includes minority public securities, funds, and co-investments. [ev:michael-hyatt-0257] [ev:michael-hyatt-0404] [ev:michael-hyatt-0232]

His mandate differs by vehicle. The family office balances public equities, private equity with less venture exposure, and operating-company acquisitions; Northleaf work favors later-stage, larger, profitable companies; direct startup investing can begin earlier but demands greater founder conviction and involvement. [ev:michael-hyatt-0413] [ev:michael-hyatt-0230] [ev:michael-hyatt-0321]

He is skeptical of venture capital as a standalone asset class because outcomes depend heavily on rare winners, few firms outperform public equities, and the winners are difficult to identify prospectively. He prefers private equity’s more repeatable three-to-four-times objective and requires a premium over public markets for illiquidity, risk, and operational work. [ev:michael-hyatt-0231] [ev:michael-hyatt-0233] [ev:michael-hyatt-0234] [ev:michael-hyatt-0236] [ev:michael-hyatt-0469]

## Distinctive / doesn't-fit-the-taxonomy

Hyatt explicitly separates entrepreneurship from investing: traits that helped him create wealth initially produced poor investment choices, while investing requires patience, humility, selectivity, and risk-adjusted judgment. He accepts missing exceptional opportunities as the price of discipline. [ev:michael-hyatt-0119] [ev:michael-hyatt-0226] [ev:michael-hyatt-0317] [ev:michael-hyatt-0324]

He describes a barbell between ordinary market returns and the higher potential return from owning companies, but the family office’s overriding posture is wealth preservation rather than aggressive risk maximization. [ev:michael-hyatt-0229] [ev:michael-hyatt-0319] [ev:michael-hyatt-0517]

His AI-assisted screening is operationally distinctive: structured research on founders, markets, competitors, crowding, and comparable-company success narrows roughly 100 inbound decks to about seven calls. AI supports synthesis and triage, but he does not trust it to select public stocks. [ev:michael-hyatt-0248] [ev:michael-hyatt-0249] [ev:michael-hyatt-0253] [ev:michael-hyatt-0411]

A tentative inferred diligence sequence is therefore: confirm identity and character; test coachability under challenge; establish customer budget and repeat expansion; examine market quality and broad competition; verify IP and differentiation; model margins, cash needs, and downside; inspect terms and ownership; then decide whether Hyatt’s active contribution justifies the time. This is a synthesis of his stated practices, not a formally published checklist.
