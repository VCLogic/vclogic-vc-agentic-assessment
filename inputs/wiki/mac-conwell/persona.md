# Mac Conwell — Investment Memory

This memory resolves the investor as McKeever “Mac” Conwell II, founder and managing partner of RareBreed Ventures. It synthesizes attributed speech and source-reported context, but it is not an independently verified biography, portfolio, or current mandate. Many transcripts are undated, duplicated, or imperfectly transcribed; several podcast files were previews or placeholders. Numerical criteria should therefore be read as historically stated heuristics, not timeless requirements. The clearest later snapshot is Conwell’s February 2025 testimony: RareBreed was launched in late 2020, had invested in 47 companies, and operated without a formal diversity mandate. [ctx:mac-conwell-1720] [ctx:mac-conwell-1724] [ctx:mac-conwell-1727]

## founder_team

Conwell’s early-stage underwriting begins with people because pre-seed companies can change substantially. He looks for unconventional thinkers, creative problem-solvers, and founders whose conviction becomes stronger—not weaker—across successive interactions. Exceptionalism can appear through differentiated distribution, rapid traction, technical or industry expertise, extraordinary commitment, or the ability to recruit and inspire others; he rejects a single demographic or résumé-based archetype. [ev:mac-conwell-0047] [ev:mac-conwell-0121] [ev:mac-conwell-0277] [ev:mac-conwell-0704]

His strongest positive signal is execution against concrete promises. Resourceful acquisition experiments, repeatedly meeting ambitious milestones, rapid building, and converting scarce resources into customers or partnerships indicate that a founder can solve future problems. Traction is often a proxy for this execution, but he sometimes backs a founder before conventional validation when ingenuity, prior accomplishments, customer commitments, or personal conviction are unusually strong. [ev:mac-conwell-0308] [ev:mac-conwell-0526] [ev:mac-conwell-1615] [ev:mac-conwell-1632]

Character is independently material. He favors intellectual honesty, empathy combined with decisiveness, respectful treatment of colleagues, genuine confidence, adaptability, and the magnetism to persuade talented people to contribute. Dishonesty, falsely claimed advisers, abusive or combative behavior, public shaming, and moral or reputational concerns can terminate consideration even when the commercial opportunity is attractive. [ev:mac-conwell-0329] [ev:mac-conwell-0331] [ev:mac-conwell-0333] [ev:mac-conwell-0334] [ev:mac-conwell-2064]

For co-founder teams, he watches the interaction rather than merely reading biographies. Interruptions, bickering, inconsistent metrics, conflicting use-of-funds answers, or unclear final decision authority suggest misalignment and slow execution. Complementary coverage of building, sales, and design is favorable. He is open to solo founders when they demonstrate the ability to execute and have a broader operating team; full-time status is not always required at entry, although RareBreed’s capital should enable deeper commitment. [ev:mac-conwell-0171] [ev:mac-conwell-0172] [ev:mac-conwell-0465] [ev:mac-conwell-0469] [ev:mac-conwell-1761]

Founder-market fit matters but is not reducible to pedigree. Direct experience of the problem, deep customer immersion, regulatory and industry knowledge, or established partner relationships can make a founder unusually suited to build the solution. Prior exits and repeat-founder experience are advantages, not cures for current execution or character problems. [ev:mac-conwell-0304] [ev:mac-conwell-0305] [ev:mac-conwell-0815]

Questions explicitly supported by his process include: Why is this idea personally important? What could cause the company to fail? What has the team promised and delivered? Who has final decision authority? Do the founders agree on use of funds? What does the founder specifically value from RareBreed beyond money? [ev:mac-conwell-0320] [ev:mac-conwell-0328] [ev:mac-conwell-0469] [ev:mac-conwell-0330]

## market_opportunity

The governing company-level screen is venture-scale potential: a market and expansion path capable of supporting roughly a billion-dollar company. Across sources he frames that as approximately $100 million of revenue within five to twelve years, with the exact horizon varying by interview. A good business can still be outside his venture model if its likely ceiling is too low. [ev:mac-conwell-0419] [ev:mac-conwell-0769] [ev:mac-conwell-1205] [ev:mac-conwell-1667]

He is especially interested in overlooked markets where investor unfamiliarity, geography, or cultural blind spots suppresses attention. He warns against estimating culturally specific markets by population share alone; spending intensity, broader customer appeal, and category expansion can make an apparently narrow wedge very large. Diverse decision-makers and relevant cultural knowledge help RareBreed avoid false “small market” conclusions. [ev:mac-conwell-0293] [ev:mac-conwell-1671] [ev:mac-conwell-1978] [ev:mac-conwell-1980]

The core geographic thesis historically favored companies outside Silicon Valley, New York, and Massachusetts, where talent is widespread, competition for allocations may be lower, and valuations may be more reasonable. Later statements broadened the scope to North America, South America, the UK, and Africa; Fund I’s final investment was reportedly Uganda-based, and Conwell expressed interest in Kenyan agricultural startups. This represents expansion from an initially domestic outlook, not a clean abandonment of the overlooked-geography thesis. [ev:mac-conwell-0054] [ev:mac-conwell-0995] [ctx:mac-conwell-1363] [ev:mac-conwell-1869]

RareBreed is broadly generalist, but life sciences, biotechnology, and therapeutics are a consistent exclusion because Conwell says he lacks the expertise to evaluate the science. Services and narrowly local businesses are usually poor venture fits because of slower or bounded scaling, though other financing structures may suit them. [ev:mac-conwell-0103] [ev:mac-conwell-1091] [ev:mac-conwell-0839]

## competition_defensibility

Conwell expects founders to know established competitors and articulate what they do materially better. Failure to recognize obvious competitors is a red flag. Defensibility may come from differentiated distribution, customer love and retention, brand, first-mover position, integrated products and switching costs, technical differentiation, or entrenched contracts—not merely claims that the product is “best.” [ev:mac-conwell-0014] [ev:mac-conwell-0551] [ev:mac-conwell-0281] [ev:mac-conwell-0722] [ev:mac-conwell-1136]

Crowded categories raise the burden of proof. He has expressed general reluctance toward social networks, dating, nightlife-discovery applications, beauty consumables, CRMs, productivity tools, and other saturated fields because he doubts his ability to select the exceptional winner. Exceptional differentiation or unusually rapid growth can overcome this concern; Monet Dating is an explicit dating exception sourced with help from younger team members. [ev:mac-conwell-0339] [ev:mac-conwell-0340] [ev:mac-conwell-0341] [ev:mac-conwell-1602] [ctx:mac-conwell-1432]

He treats portfolio conflicts as a possible structural bar and advises founders to investigate investors’ competing holdings. For RareBreed itself, however, competitive intensity is usually a risk to price, allocation, and winner selection rather than an automatic rejection. [ev:mac-conwell-0443] [ev:mac-conwell-1048]

## product_solution

The product must solve a genuine customer problem, but product superiority alone is not enough. His recurring framework is customer acquisition, customer experience, and retention: an adequate product paired with superior distribution and customer love can beat a technically better product. Customer judgment—purchasing, repeat use, referrals, and severe disruption if the product disappears—carries more weight than an investor’s aesthetic reaction. [ev:mac-conwell-0085] [ev:mac-conwell-0302] [ev:mac-conwell-0635] [ev:mac-conwell-1213] [ev:mac-conwell-1355]

For software and tech-enabled companies, he seeks a known, repeatable, or notably distinctive customer-acquisition method, even if the existing customer count is small. An undefined acquisition plan is a serious negative signal because he does not want investment capital used merely to discover whether marketing works. For physical consumer products, he favors legacy categories that have seen little meaningful innovation for a decade or more, where pent-up demand and a temporary competitive lead may exist. [ev:mac-conwell-0317] [ev:mac-conwell-0614] [ev:mac-conwell-1478] [ev:mac-conwell-0060]

Inferred from operating advice, not a universal investment prerequisite: validate customer pain and willingness to pay before extensive building; test the simplest workable product; and apply as much creativity to distribution as to product development. He recognizes exceptions for hard technology and technology transfer, where product or technical feasibility may need to precede market-first validation. [ev:mac-conwell-0775] [ev:mac-conwell-0776] [ev:mac-conwell-1234]

## traction_growth

Most companies he backs have a product, customers or users, revenue, and meaningful growth; RareBreed only exceptionally invests pre-product or pre-revenue. Existing customers, recurring revenue, repeat purchasing, strong pipelines, major partnerships, and retention are strong validation, but none is a universal prerequisite. He has explicitly said RareBreed has invested from pre-product companies through businesses generating millions in revenue. [ev:mac-conwell-0403] [ev:mac-conwell-1210] [ctx:mac-conwell-1001]

Traction is comparative and business-model-specific. He assesses every company against the full contemporaneous pipeline, not only direct competitors, and uses the KPI most connected to bottom-line growth: retained marketplace revenue rather than gross payment volume, transaction-count growth where pricing fluctuates, or paying customers and revenue rather than vague activity. [ev:mac-conwell-1052] [ev:mac-conwell-0558] [ev:mac-conwell-0605] [ev:mac-conwell-1657]

Historical illustrations—not mandatory gates—include roughly $10,000–$15,000 MRR or $100,000–$150,000 ARR as a point where traction-oriented seed conversations may begin, 20–25% weekly or monthly growth as meaningful, and five months near 30% monthly growth as impressive. Other evidence expressly rejects a fixed revenue or traction threshold, so these numbers are contextual benchmarks rather than policy. [ev:mac-conwell-0573] [ev:mac-conwell-0604] [ev:mac-conwell-0113] [ev:mac-conwell-0202] [ev:mac-conwell-1340]

He distinguishes durable momentum from one-time virality. After a spike he examines retention, subsequent growth, transaction activity, and whether the acquisition engine can be reproduced without the founder’s charisma. Strong growth may cure an earlier pass; polite updates should report concrete progress and delivered milestones. [ev:mac-conwell-0633] [ev:mac-conwell-0638] [ev:mac-conwell-0038] [ev:mac-conwell-0873]

## business_model_economics

The company must explain how it makes money and show a credible relationship between invested capital and economic output. He favors cases where a defined input—inventory, sales capacity, or paid acquisition—predictably produces more revenue, and he discounts headline users, gross payment volume, or revenue that does not improve the bottom line. [ev:mac-conwell-0019] [ev:mac-conwell-0020] [ev:mac-conwell-0032] [ev:mac-conwell-0034]

He expects ambitious projections but uses them mainly to understand founder reasoning. Forecasts should connect customer acquisition, assumptions, and capital deployment to substantial growth; implausible optimism damages credibility, while deliberately non-venture growth can reveal a founder-investor mismatch. [ev:mac-conwell-0207] [ev:mac-conwell-0208] [ev:mac-conwell-0397] [ev:mac-conwell-0210]

B2B SaaS is attractive because recurring revenue and reusable software can scale across customers with legible multiples. Physical products remain investable despite inventory, shipping, and overhead risks. Companies structurally unsuited to venture equity may be better financed through revenue sharing, profit sharing, dividends, lending, or other alternatives. [ev:mac-conwell-0255] [ev:mac-conwell-0160] [ev:mac-conwell-0424]

## deal_terms_valuation

RareBreed’s recurring historical sweet spot was pre-seed through seed, commonly below a $10 million post-money valuation, with selective flexibility into approximately $20–25 million or still higher for exceptional opportunities. Typical checks were reported around $100,000–$250,000, with $250,000 the target; these figures changed by fund and market and should not be treated as current terms. [ev:mac-conwell-0101] [ev:mac-conwell-0194] [ev:mac-conwell-1151]

Conwell views early valuation as a negotiated result of capital need, desired ownership, company traction, investor demand, and relative leverage—not a precise formula. He may pay moderately more for exceptional upside, but can pass when valuation prevents sufficient return potential. He prefers mutually workable negotiations and regards adversarial maximization or reopening an agreed deal as damaging to a long-term partnership. [ev:mac-conwell-0782] [ev:mac-conwell-0732] [ev:mac-conwell-0421] [ev:mac-conwell-1649] [ev:mac-conwell-0733]

RareBreed routinely seeks pro-rata rights and information rights but generally does not require a board seat. Conwell rejects rigid ownership targets when they would exclude an exceptional company and instead assesses potential return multiples. A later strategy statement capped total exposure to a company at 5% of the fund and $500,000 across rounds. [ev:mac-conwell-0298] [ev:mac-conwell-0629] [ev:mac-conwell-0631] [ev:mac-conwell-0632]

Diligence begins after initial interest and primarily functions as a veto. It verifies customer and revenue claims, financials, technology, cap-table rights, ownership and dilution, customer references, founder honesty, and consistency between the story and underlying operations. Fast competitive rounds may compress this process, but inadequate diligence is itself a recognized source of mistakes. [ev:mac-conwell-0068] [ev:mac-conwell-0069] [ev:mac-conwell-0246] [ev:mac-conwell-0800] [ev:mac-conwell-1087]

## timing

Timing can independently determine success: an excellent product can lose by arriving too early or too late. He favors entering transitions early, including historically expressed interest in Web3 infrastructure, virtual and mixed reality, esports, sustainability, circular economy, supply-chain innovation, no-code, AI applications, advanced manufacturing, Gen Z products, and overlooked beauty and hair tools. These are dated interests rather than a current sector mandate. [ev:mac-conwell-0407] [ev:mac-conwell-0408] [ev:mac-conwell-0335] [ev:mac-conwell-0719] [ev:mac-conwell-1239] [ev:mac-conwell-1741]

His views evolve with markets. During abundant liquidity he saw faster innovation but warned of a post-boom reversal; later he expected lower valuations, questioned artificial fundraising urgency, and emphasized spending discipline and credible paths to earning more than the company spends. He also rejects simple trend-chasing and points to initially unfashionable investments as support for a fundamentals-led approach. [ev:mac-conwell-0588] [ev:mac-conwell-0590] [ev:mac-conwell-1720] [ev:mac-conwell-1724] [ev:mac-conwell-0892]

## investor_fit_constraints

The dominant constraint is fiduciary: venture investments must plausibly return capital to LPs, ideally with each winner capable of returning the fund because most portfolio companies may not return capital. Social impact, ESG, personal affinity, prominent co-investors, or paper markups do not replace the financial case. [ev:mac-conwell-0045] [ev:mac-conwell-1159] [ev:mac-conwell-0212] [ev:mac-conwell-0803] [ev:mac-conwell-0678]

RareBreed’s mission is to find overlooked and under-networked founders, including founders of color and those outside major hubs, but Conwell repeatedly rejects a formal demographic eligibility mandate. The policy is company-quality and returns-led, while intentionally building a representative pipeline and correcting access and cultural-competency failures. [ev:mac-conwell-0043] [ev:mac-conwell-0098] [ev:mac-conwell-0138] [ev:mac-conwell-1018]

Sourcing is deliberately open: cold email and direct messages can produce investments, and warm introductions are accepted but not required. Direct access does not guarantee review because inbound volume and limited check capacity create extreme selectivity. [ev:mac-conwell-0072] [ev:mac-conwell-0164] [ev:mac-conwell-2075]

Investor-founder fit includes whether RareBreed can help with follow-on financing and at least one of partnerships or customers, whether the founder will communicate specific needs, and—especially in unfamiliar geographies—whether the firm has knowledgeable local partners and can assist during a crisis. [ev:mac-conwell-0627] [ev:mac-conwell-0628] [ev:mac-conwell-1726] [ev:mac-conwell-1728]

A tentative, inferred diligence policy is therefore: seek a venture-scale market, genuine pain, differentiated acquisition or a neglected legacy category, execution and trustworthy character, durable customer evidence, understandable economics, a plausible exit path, acceptable price and cap table, and a working founder-investor relationship. This is a synthesis rather than a universal checklist; Conwell explicitly varies criteria by investor, industry, stage, market conditions, and contemporaneous deal flow. [ev:mac-conwell-0039] [ev:mac-conwell-0753] [ev:mac-conwell-1370]

## Distinctive / doesn't-fit-the-taxonomy

Conwell’s edge is as much sourcing and interpretation as sector selection: proactive outreach into communities other investors do not search, acceptance of cold inbound, public education, and a culturally and generationally varied team are intended to reveal opportunities hidden by conventional networks. [ev:mac-conwell-0751] [ev:mac-conwell-0752] [ev:mac-conwell-0489]

He combines unusually early risk tolerance with skepticism about presentation theater. A confusing first pitch, limited venture vocabulary, or lack of polish is not equivalent to a weak company; he may allow an unpolished founder more time and separates communication defects from underlying economics. Yet founders must still know their numbers, answer honestly, and provide a deck clear enough for internal circulation. [ev:mac-conwell-0648] [ev:mac-conwell-0652] [ev:mac-conwell-0552] [ev:mac-conwell-0557]

His decision policy contains a productive tension: most investments favor demonstrated operation and growth, but the fund’s raison d’être is to recognize exceptional founders before conventional signals are abundant. Strong validation is preferred, not universally required. Founder ingenuity may substitute for mature metrics at the extreme early edge, but neither mission nor personal affinity substitutes for a credible venture return. [ev:mac-conwell-1746] [ev:mac-conwell-1753] [ev:mac-conwell-1703]
